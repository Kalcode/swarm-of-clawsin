Distilling a Fast Mind

Tonight a big, slow model plays practice worlds. Tomorrow a small, fast model learns from its best moves. This page walks through how that works, and where two classic game-AI ideas, FSM and GOAP, end up inside the model.

1 · The teacher playsgpt-oss 20B thinks for creatures in practice worlds all night and writes down what it saw, chose and caused.
2 · We keep the good movesMoves that worked become lessons. Mistakes, no-ops and anything from Gemini are thrown out.
3 · The student learns themA ~1.7B model is fine-tuned on those lessons with LoRA on this Mac, then joins the live world.

Chapter 1 · cheap behaviour

A finite state machine: rules that run every tick

A finite state machine (FSM) is the oldest trick in game AI. A character is always in exactly one state, and a few rules decide which. It costs almost nothing, so it can run every tick for every character. Our animals already work this way. Below is their real rule list from simulation.py: checked top to bottom each tick, first match wins.

State: graze

  1. 1An untamed animal sees a stranger within reachwary: flee
  2. 2Its owner is within 6 tiles with food it likesfollow
  3. 3It is nightrest
  4. 4It is more than 3 tiles from homego home
  5. 5Otherwisegraze

Creatures have one rule like this too, a survival reflex: a starving body eats what it carries, or berries within reach, with no thought at all.

An FSM is fast and predictable, but it only does what someone wrote down. A sheep never invents a better way to flee.

Chapter 2 · planning

GOAP: work backwards from a goal

Goal-Oriented Action Planning (GOAP) gives a character a goal instead of a state. Every action lists what it needs and what it gives. A planner chains actions backwards from the goal until everything needed is already in hand. The 2005 shooter F.E.A.R. made it famous: its soldiers planned their own flanking moves.

Here is a tiny planner running on our real recipes, from where a creature named Holt actually stood in a test world. He carries 6 plant fiber, 2 sticks and 1 stone. An oak tree is 5 steps southwest and a boulder 4 steps southwest. Pick a goal:

    Real GOAP searches for the cheapest plan (usually with A*). This demo chains backwards greedily, which is enough for a recipe tree. Recipes: 3 plant fiber → rope; stick + stone + rope → stone axe; 3 sticks + 2 stones → campfire; 2 sticks + rope + plant fiber → fishing rod; a log → 2 planks (needs an axe); 4 planks → workbench.

    A planner like this is about forty lines and answers instantly. But it only knows the recipes we typed in. Our creatures discover recipes, invent things, read signs and talk each other into plans. A hand-written planner can't do any of that.

    One Mac runs about 20 creatures' minds today. The goal is 80 or more. A model several times smaller could think several times faster, if it can learn to plan as well as the big one. That is what distillation is for.

    Chapter 4 · teacher and student

    Distillation: a small model copies a big one's good moves

    Distillation means training a small student model on answers from a big teacher. The student doesn't need to know everything the teacher knows. It only needs to answer the way the teacher does for this one job: being a creature in our world.

    Chapter 5 · one lesson up close

    A lesson is a conversation with the right answer filled in

    Every think is a short chat. The system message holds the world's rules, about 1,000 tokens and the same for everyone. The user message is the creature's senses and memories right now. The assistant message is the answer. A lesson keeps all three. Training grades the student only on the answer.

    system · the world's rules≈1,000 tokens
    The first lines (real) 
    You are a living creature in a small wild world. This is not a game you are playing: you only know what your own senses tell you right now, plus your memories. Other creatures live here too, each with a mind of its own. Sound carries only about 5 steps, so to talk with someone, walk to them first.

Each turn choose ONE action and reply with ONLY a JSON object of this shape:
{"thought": "...", "action": "...", "target": "...", "items": [], "text": "", "count": 1, "goal": "", "speech": ""}
…then every action, and the rules of the world.

    Chapter 6 · choosing lessons

    Only the good moves become lessons

    A student copies everything it's shown, mistakes included. So the logbook is filtered before training. These seven moves are real, from the live world today. Flip the rules and watch what the student would learn.

    “Counts twice” means the lesson appears twice; “counts half” means it's kept half the time. The exact weights get tuned. Whole practice worlds are also held back unseen, as the student's exam.

    Chapter 7 · how a model learns

    Guess the next token, measure the surprise, nudge

    A model reads and writes in tokens, small pieces of text. To answer, it guesses one token at a time, giving every possible next token a probability. Training walks through each lesson's answer and, at every token, asks how surprised the student was by the teacher's choice. That surprise is the loss. Then it nudges the model's numbers so the teacher's choice becomes a little more likely next time.

    {"thought":"Six fiber makes rope…","action":"?

    Illustrative numbers, not a measurement. The teacher wrote craft. Surprise is −ln(probability of the right token), so 9% gives a surprise of 2.4 and 81% gives 0.2. Training averages this over every answer token of every lesson, then repeats for a few hundred to a few thousand steps.

    Chapter 8 · LoRA

    Learning without rewriting the whole brain

    A model is billions of numbers arranged in big grids (matrices). Changing all of them is a full fine-tune. It is slow, and it needs memory for extra copies of every number. LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes the original grid W and trains two thin strips beside it, B and A, whose product is added on top. The strips' width is the rank.

    One 2,048 × 2,048 layer, full fine-tune
    4,194,304 numbers change
    The same layer with LoRA
    32,768 numbers learn · 0.78%

    Main layers in a ~1.7B Qwen are about 2,048 numbers wide. Rank 8 to 16 is the usual start. Higher ranks can learn more and need more memory. Because only the strips train, it fits on this Mac beside the world (target: about 12 GB).

    When training is done, the strips are fused: W + B×A is computed once and saved as one ordinary model, ~/models/swarm-student-<date>, which oMLX serves like any other.

    Chapter 9 · knowing when to stop

    Memorizing is not learning

    Train long enough and the student starts memorizing its lessons word for word. Surprise on the lessons keeps falling, but on practice worlds it has never seen, surprise starts to rise. That is overfitting. So we hold back whole practice worlds, not random lines, and keep the checkpoint where surprise on them was lowest. Random lines would leak: the same world's next tick looks almost identical.

    Chapter 10 · the plan, step by step

    From one night of play to a new mind

    TONIGHT

    1 · Practice worlds

    Fresh worlds with new seeds, 6 creatures each, 2,400 ticks (two in-game days). They run turn-based, so speed doesn't matter. gpt-oss 20B thinks for every creature, on your PC when it's free and on this Mac meanwhile, with all the harness layers on.
    MORNING

    2 · Keep the good moves

    export_training.py keeps only gpt-oss and Qwen moves that worked and changed something, weights them, removes duplicates and holds back whole worlds for the exam.
    MORNING

    3 · Train with LoRA

    train_student.sh runs mlx-lm, made for Apple silicon. The ~1.7B Qwen3 base stays frozen while the strips learn. The live world slows or pauses for those hours.
    AFTER

    4 · Fuse

    The strips are folded into the base: one model folder in ~/models that oMLX can serve.
    LIVE

    5 · Half the creatures

    ADOPT gives half of one model's creatures the student's mind. review_world.py --split compares the halves: success, no-ops, collapses, things made and thoughts per minute.
    AGAIN

    6 · Better moves, better lessons

    The student's own best games become new lessons, and it trains again. This loop is called rejection-sampling fine-tuning.

    One trap to get right: the live brain asks for answers with “thinking” turned off and a strict JSON shape. The lessons must look exactly like what the student will see when it's serving, or it learns a format it never gets.

    Chapter 11 · the whole picture

    Where FSM and GOAP end up

    Games run cheap rules every tick and consult something smarter only at decision points. That is the shape we're building toward. The rules stay as code. The planning moves into a small, fast model that learned it from watching a big one plan.

    Words, for later

    Glossary

    Token
    A small piece of text, often part of a word. Models read and write in tokens; Holt's prompt is about 1,400 of them.
    Weights
    The model's numbers. A “1.7B” model has about 1.7 billion of them.
    Fine-tuning
    Training an already-trained model a little more, on your own examples.
    Distillation
    Fine-tuning a small student on a big teacher's answers.
    LoRA
    Low-rank adaptation: freeze the weights, train thin add-on strips.
    Rank
    How wide the LoRA strips are. More rank, more capacity, more memory.
    Loss
    The student's average surprise at the right answer. Training pushes it down.
    Overfitting
    Memorizing the lessons instead of learning the skill.
    Held-out set
    Examples the student never trains on, kept to test it. Ours are whole practice worlds.
    Fuse
    Fold the trained strips into the weights to get one ordinary model.
    4-bit
    Storing each weight in 4 bits instead of 16, so a model takes a quarter of the memory.
    FSM
    Finite state machine: one state at a time, rules pick it. Cheap enough for every tick.
    GOAP
    Goal-oriented action planning: chain actions backwards from a goal.
    Rejection sampling
    Keep only the attempts that turned out well and train on those.
